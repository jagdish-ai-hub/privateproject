A **SIP (Systematic Investment Plan)** is a way of investing a fixed amount in a mutual fund at regular intervals, most often every month. You choose the scheme, the amount and the date, and the money is debited automatically from your bank account.

## How a SIP works

Each instalment buys units at that day's [NAV](/learn/what-is-nav-in-mutual-funds/). If you invest ₹5,000 when the NAV is ₹50, you get 100 units. Next month, if the NAV is ₹40, ₹5,000 buys 125 units. After two months you own 225 units for ₹10,000, an average cost of about ₹44.44 per unit, lower than the simple average of the two NAVs (₹45). This is called **rupee-cost averaging**.

It works in your favour when prices dip and you keep investing. It does not remove risk: if prices keep falling, every instalment buys at a loss for a while.

## Why many people use it

- **Discipline**: the investment happens automatically, without trying to time the market.
- **Small start**: many schemes accept SIPs from ₹100 to ₹500 a month.
- **Flexibility**: you can stop, pause or change the amount, and a [step-up SIP](/tools/step-up-sip-calculator/) raises the amount every year as your income grows.

## What a SIP cannot do

It cannot guarantee returns, and it cannot make a poor fund good. The result depends on the fund you pick and the length of time you stay invested. A short SIP in an equity fund can easily end below what you put in.

## Measuring SIP returns

Because every instalment is invested for a different length of time, a SIP's return is measured with **XIRR**, not a simple percentage. The screener's "SIP 3Y" and "SIP 5Y" columns show the XIRR of a monthly SIP over the last 3 or 5 years. See [CAGR vs XIRR vs absolute return](/learn/cagr-xirr-absolute-return/).
