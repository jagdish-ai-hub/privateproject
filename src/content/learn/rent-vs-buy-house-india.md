Whether to buy a home or keep renting is one of the largest money decisions most people make, and it is often made on feeling. "Rent is money down the drain" and "a home is the best investment" are both slogans. The better answer comes from putting numbers on both sides.

## The question to ask

Buying a home is not just spending; it is also **putting a large amount of money in one place**. The right comparison is not "rent versus EMI" but: **if I rent and invest everything I would otherwise have put into the house, who is richer after N years?**

That means counting on the buyer's side:

- the **down payment** and **stamp duty and registration**, paid on day one (stamp duty varies by state, and often adds several percent of the price);
- the **EMI** every month, in which most of the early payments are interest;
- **maintenance, society charges and property tax**, every year;
- the **selling costs and tax** when you sell, and any loan still left.

And on the renter's side:

- the **rent**, which usually rises every year;
- the **investments** built from the down payment and from whatever the renter saves each month compared with the buyer's monthly outgo.

The [rent vs buy calculator](/tools/rent-vs-buy-calculator/) does exactly this, with both people always spending the same total each month.

## The number that matters most: rental yield

**Rental yield** is yearly rent divided by the home's price. In many Indian cities the yield on residential property is commonly quoted as a low single-digit percentage, much lower than home loan interest rates. Check what a similar home actually rents for in your area.

When the yield is low, owning costs far more each month than renting, and the extra money the renter invests grows. For buying to win, the home's price has to grow enough to make up the gap, and that is a bet about your particular area. The calculator reports your yield and the **monthly rent at which buying would break even**: if the rent you pay is above it, buying comes out ahead on your assumptions.

## The other numbers that decide it

- **How long you stay.** The costs of buying and selling (stamp duty, brokerage, tax) are paid once, so a short stay rarely pays them back. The longer you stay, the more they spread out.
- **Price growth of the home.** This is the biggest unknown. Use your own area's history, be cautious, and test a few values.
- **What you would earn investing instead.** If you are comfortable investing in equity funds over many years, the renter's side grows faster. If you would keep the money in a bank account, it grows slower. See [what is a SIP](/learn/what-is-sip/) and [SIP vs lumpsum](/learn/sip-vs-lumpsum/).
- **The home loan rate and tenure.** A higher rate makes the early EMIs mostly interest, which builds little ownership at first.
- **Tax.** Selling a home and selling investments are both taxed on the gain, and the rules have changed over time. See [mutual fund taxation](/learn/mutual-fund-taxation-india/) for the investment side, and check the current rules for property.

## What a calculator cannot tell you

Numbers are one input. Owning offers things money alone does not measure: stability, freedom to modify the home, protection from rent hikes and a place that is yours. Renting offers freedom to move for work, a smaller concentration of money in one asset and a lower cost of changing your mind. A good decision weighs both the numbers and what matters to you.

## A sensible way to use the calculator

1. Enter the actual price and the actual rent for a similar home near you.
2. Use a cautious price growth and a cautious investment return, then try higher and lower ones.
3. Look at the **break-even rent** and the **year buying catches up**, not just the headline result.
4. If the answer flips with small changes in the assumptions, treat it as close, and decide on the other factors.

*This guide is general information, not financial advice. Property and investment returns are uncertain, and tax rules change; check the current rules for your situation.*
