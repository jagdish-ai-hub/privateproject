**NAV (Net Asset Value)** is the price of one unit of a mutual fund. It is the number you see next to a fund's name and the price at which you buy or sell units.

## How NAV is calculated

At the end of each business day the fund house adds up the market value of everything the scheme holds, subtracts its liabilities and accrued expenses, and divides by the number of units in issue:

**NAV = (value of assets − liabilities) ÷ units outstanding**

Because the fund's yearly [expense ratio](/learn/expense-ratio-and-exit-load/) is deducted a little each day, a published NAV is already after costs.

## A low NAV is not a bargain

A common mistake is to prefer a fund with a NAV of ₹15 over one at ₹300 because it "looks cheaper". NAV is not a share price in that sense. When you invest an amount, you simply get more units at a low NAV and fewer at a high NAV, and your total value is the same. Two funds that grow by the same percentage give you the same result whatever their NAVs are.

New fund offers usually start at ₹10 per unit. That does not make them cheaper than a fund whose NAV has grown to ₹100 over many years.

## NAV and returns

The return of a fund is the change in its NAV over a period. If a NAV rises from ₹100 to ₹121 in two years, the fund returned 21% in total, which is about 10% a year. See [CAGR vs XIRR vs absolute return](/learn/cagr-xirr-absolute-return/) for how to express this.

For funds that pay out [IDCW](/learn/growth-vs-idcw-mutual-funds/), the NAV falls by the amount distributed, so comparing NAVs alone understates what the investor earned. Funds can also change their unit value in a split; this site detects those and adjusts its calculations so that the change is not mistaken for a loss.
