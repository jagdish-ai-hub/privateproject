A **recurring deposit (RD)** and a **[SIP](/learn/what-is-sip/)** both take a fixed amount every month. The RD pays a fixed interest rate; the SIP buys mutual fund units at whatever the market price is. That single difference drives everything else.

## Side by side

| | SIP (equity fund) | RD (post office, 5 years) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Return | Not fixed | 6.7% a year (October–December 2026), fixed for the term |
| Risk | Value moves with the market | Government-backed, maturity amount known |
| Term | No fixed term | 5 years (can be extended) |
| Stopping | Anytime, no penalty | Early closure only under the rules |
| Tax | On gains when you sell | Interest taxed at slab rate |

## ₹5,000 a month for 5 years

Both put in ₹3,00,000. The [post office RD calculator](/schemes/post-office-rd-calculator/) gives about **₹3,56,829** at maturity, a certain amount. A [SIP](/tools/sip-calculator/) at an assumed 10% a year would reach about ₹3,90,412, and at 12% about ₹4,12,432, but the real figure depends on the market in those five years and could be lower than the RD.

## When an RD fits better

- The goal is 1 to 5 years away and the amount matters: a down payment, school fees, a wedding.
- You would lose sleep over a fall just before the goal.
- You are building the habit of saving and want a known result.

## When a SIP fits better

- The goal is more than 5 years away.
- You can leave the money invested through a fall without stopping.
- You want inflation-beating growth and accept that it is not guaranteed.

## Using both

A common approach is to split monthly savings: an RD for near goals and a SIP for long ones. As a SIP goal gets close, moving money from the equity fund into an RD or a low-risk debt fund protects what has been built.
