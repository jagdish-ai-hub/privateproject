## Active funds

A fund manager and research team pick investments to try to beat a benchmark index. You pay for that effort through a higher [expense ratio](/learn/expense-ratio-and-exit-load/). If the manager picks well enough to cover the extra cost, you gain; if not, you would have been better off in an index fund.

## Index funds

An index fund simply copies an index. There is no stock-picking, so costs are low. Its return will be the index's return minus costs, so it will never beat the index and rarely trails it by much. Index funds are available in both [Direct and Regular plans](/learn/direct-vs-regular-mutual-funds/), and you buy them like any other fund, including through a SIP.

## ETFs

An **exchange-traded fund** also tracks an index (or gold, silver or bonds) but is bought and sold on a stock exchange like a share.

| | Index fund | ETF |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How you buy | From the fund house at NAV | On the exchange at the market price |
| Price | End-of-day NAV | Changes through the day |
| Needs a demat account | No | Yes |
| SIP | Easy | Possible through some brokers |
| Extra cost | None | Brokerage and the gap between buy and sell price |
| Liquidity | Redeem at NAV | Depends on trading volume |

## What to compare

- **Expense ratio**: the main difference between funds tracking the same index.
- **Tracking error and tracking difference**: how far the fund's return falls from the index.
- **Size and trading volume** (for ETFs): thin trading means a wider gap between the buy and sell price.

## Unit splits

ETFs occasionally split their units, for example 10 new units for each old unit, so the NAV drops to a tenth overnight without any loss to investors. Raw NAV data makes this look like a crash. This site detects such splits and adjusts the history, and says so on the fund page.

## Choosing

Active and passive funds are not rivals so much as tools. Many investors hold both: a low-cost index fund as the core and a few active funds.
