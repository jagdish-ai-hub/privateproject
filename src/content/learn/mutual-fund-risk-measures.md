Returns tell you what a fund earned. Risk measures tell you what it took to earn it. This site shows four, each calculated from the last three years of daily NAVs.

## Volatility (standard deviation)

Take each day's percentage change in NAV, measure how widely those changes scatter, and scale it up to a yearly figure (× √252 trading days). A fund with 20% volatility swings much more than one with 8%. Equity funds are more volatile than debt funds, small-cap funds more than large-cap funds. Compare volatility only within a category.

## Sharpe ratio

**Sharpe = (average annual return − risk-free rate) ÷ volatility**

It asks how much return you got above a safe alternative for each unit of volatility. This site uses a 6.5% yearly risk-free rate. A higher Sharpe ratio is better, but it can mislead when returns are very low or negative, and it treats big gains and big losses as equally "risky".

## Sortino ratio

A variation that divides by *downside* deviation, which only counts the days when the fund did worse than the risk-free rate. It does not penalise a fund for upward swings, so it is often a fairer measure for funds with uneven returns.

## Maximum drawdown

The largest peak-to-trough fall in the period. It answers a practical question: what was the worst fall an investor had to sit through? A fund with a 3-year drawdown of −35% fell by more than a third at some point, even if it finished the period higher. This is often the number that decides whether an investor stays invested or sells in a panic.

## How to use them together

- Compare **within the same category**. Comparing a liquid fund with a small-cap fund is meaningless.
- Look at **return and risk together**: two funds with the same return but different drawdowns offer different experiences.
- Check [rolling returns](/learn/rolling-returns-explained/) for consistency.
- Remember the window: three years may not include a major fall, so a calm period can flatter a fund.

## Limits

All of these describe the past and depend on the period chosen. They do not capture risks that have not occurred yet, such as a debt fund's credit risk before a default. The riskometer on each scheme's documents is SEBI's official risk label, and should be read alongside these figures.
